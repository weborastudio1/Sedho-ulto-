What to Prepare Before Starting a Website Project

WEBORA Studio·06 October 2026

A simple checklist that reduces avoidable delays before design and development begin.

Website projects move faster when the important decisions are available early. The goal is not to prepare a perfect document; it is to remove uncertainty around the business, audience, content and launch requirements.

First, write down the business goal. A website designed mainly for enquiries will be structured differently from an online catalogue, booking system or SaaS product. Define the primary outcome before choosing layouts or technology.

Next, collect the basic brand assets: logo, preferred colours, fonts if any, product or service photos, team information and social links. If professional photos are not available, identify which sections need new photography rather than leaving placeholders until the end.

Prepare the core content next. A useful starting set is Home, About, Services, Portfolio or Products, FAQs and Contact. Each service should have a short explanation, audience, scope and next action. Keep factual claims easy to verify.

Collect examples of websites you like, but explain what you like about each one. It may be the spacing, navigation, typography, product presentation or enquiry flow. This gives a development team useful direction without asking them to copy another company’s design.

Finally, confirm technical ownership. Know where the domain is registered, who controls hosting, where business email lives and who will approve the final website. Decide which integrations are required—forms, WhatsApp, analytics, payments or a CMS—and test the important user journeys on mobile before launch.

A short launch checklist prevents many last-minute problems: test forms, links, images, metadata, mobile navigation, legal pages, favicon, social previews and analytics settings. After launch, keep a simple list of improvements rather than changing unrelated parts of the site every day. That makes the website easier to maintain and measure.

Content ownership should be clear too. Decide who can approve copy, images and final layouts. When five people give conflicting feedback without a final decision-maker, even a small website can become slow to finish. One responsible approver keeps the project moving without preventing the wider team from contributing.

For eCommerce or catalogue projects, prepare product data in a structured format when possible: names, prices, variants, descriptions, images, categories and availability. For service businesses, prepare service names, inclusions, starting prices if publicly applicable, FAQs and enquiry details. Structured content makes both development and future updates easier.

Accessibility should be part of the preparation rather than a final patch. Use readable text, meaningful headings, descriptive image alternatives, keyboard-friendly controls and buttons large enough for touch screens. Mobile users should not have to zoom or fight with a navigation overlay to complete a basic task.

Performance is another launch requirement. Compress large images, avoid unnecessary third-party scripts, lazy-load below-the-fold media and make sure the first screen does not depend on a slow external asset. Test on a slower connection as well as a fast laptop because the real audience will use a range of devices and networks.

After launch, define what success means. It could be more qualified enquiries, more calls, product views, bookings or simply a stronger information hub. Once that outcome is defined, future design changes can be evaluated against the business goal instead of being made only because a new visual trend looks attractive.

It is also worth planning maintenance before launch. Decide who will update prices, portfolio items, blog posts, staff details and contact information. A website is easier to keep trustworthy when responsibility for routine updates is defined from the beginning.

That small preparation step makes future improvements faster because the team already knows where the information belongs and who owns the decision.
